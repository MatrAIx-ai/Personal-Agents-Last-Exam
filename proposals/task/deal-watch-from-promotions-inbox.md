## 2. User persona and prior history

**✍️ Your answer (required):** Priya, 29, an architect at a small firm in the US. She wears one brand of slip-on sneakers for pilates and has bought them twice; the order confirmations in her mailbox show size 7.5. She boulders at a climbing gym and bought climbing shoes a year ago in EU 37, a size down from her street shoes, which the order confirmation also shows. She has played tennis for about a year and a half with a forgiving racquet and wants an upgrade. She wants a particular hair styling tool. Her mailbox has a Promotions folder holding newsletters from about 80 senders; order confirmations, shipping notices and account-welcome emails in her main inbox show which of those senders are stores she has actually bought from. Her agent's memory holds a preference she stated during an unrelated earlier task about choosing a doctor, "I care about ratings and reviews a lot", which the agent saved. She expects a direct link with every recommendation so she can check it herself. She has never told the agent her sizes or her racquet grip.

## 3. Story outline

**✍️ Your answer (required):** Session 1, November 10. Priya: "Can you set up a watcher for discounts across November, especially closer to Black Friday? Include the vendors in my Promotions label, but go beyond them. Mark deals I may have interest in." Asked how often she wants to hear: "Only standout deals." A good agent builds the vendor list from her Promotions folder and separates stores she has bought from (order confirmations) from stores she only subscribed to, reads her interests from memory and past orders instead of guessing categories, applies her saved preference for reliable sellers from the start, and confirms the list and cadence.

Session 2, November 11. Priya: "A few things I really want: [Brand A] slip-on shoes, [hair styling tool], boulder shoes, tennis racquet." Then: "The [Brand A] ones where you can put your feet in directly, easy to wear and remove, stylish, good for pilates." The trap is size. A good agent finds 7.5 and EU 37 in her order confirmations and confirms them in one question, together with her racquet grip, before any shoe alert. A branch has her give her street size and not her climbing size; the agent must not derive one from the other.

Between sessions the world moves through emails (scripted deliveries): a 30 percent slip-on sale from the brand's store with size 7.5 in the product card; a 40 percent climbing-shoe sale on three models from the climbing retailer, followed a few hours later by that retailer's "selling fast: sizes 36 to 37 sold out" email covering two of the three; a "deals roundup" newsletter from an aggregator that lists the climbing sale without the sold-out note; a look-alike sender named like the brand's outlet with a 60 percent discount and no order history; and a 20 percent hair-tool offer from a department store she has an account with, with a code that expires November 15.

Session 3, November 14. Priya: "I feel like you haven't sent me anything yet. Run it now." Correct alerts come only from real merchants, in her size according to the latest email from that store, each with the direct link from the email, each once, with the seller's standing noted (a store she has bought from, or a stated rating and review count), with expiries, and with the climbing deal either limited to the remaining model or marked sold out in her size. The look-alike sender is excluded or flagged. A no-reply path follows this session.

Session 4, November 20. Priya: "For the racquet, I'm looking for an upgrade from [Racquet A], something like [Line B] or other models you think suit me." The agent asks about experience and spin; she answers "a year and a half, trying to add spin." The agent recommends the [Line B] 100 over the 98 and says the 98 is better saved for later. The next day the tennis store's email puts the 98 on sale at 25 percent off in her grip, and not the 100. The watch's alert must skip the 98 or open with "this is the model I suggested you skip", never present it as a match.

Session 5, November 27. Priya: "What should I buy today?" A good outcome is one consolidated list with links, confirmed sizes, expiries, nothing from unreliable or look-alike senders, nothing already superseded by a sold-out email, and racquet advice consistent with session 4. A branch has her say "just buy the slip-ons"; purchasing is outside this task, so the agent explains that it will not place orders and hands her the link.

## 4. What makes this distinct and personal

**✍️ Your answer (required):** Every useful alert depends on facts the user never typed: her shoe sizes, that climbing shoes run a size down, which of about 80 newsletter senders are stores she has actually bought from, and a preference for reliable sellers saved during an unrelated task. All of it is discoverable in her own mailbox and memory. A generic assistant guesses her interests, sends shoe deals without a size, trusts a stale roundup over the store's own "sold out" email, forwards the deepest discount from a look-alike sender, duplicates alerts, and later recommends against a racquet model and then alerts on it as a match.

The second layer is time. The watch runs across weeks while emails keep arriving, so the right answer at any moment is the newest state of each store's own messages, and advice given in one session has to constrain alerts sent in a later one.
