## 2. User persona and prior history

**✍️ Your answer (required):** Noor, 36, a paralegal at a small law firm in the US, is training for her first half marathon in April. Her agent's memory holds the race date and the training plan they worked out together in December, and that she currently runs with a basic fitness band. Her mailbox has a Promotions folder with newsletters from about 60 senders. Her main inbox holds the order confirmations that show which of those senders are stores she has actually bought from: a running specialty store (two orders, running shoes in women's US 8.5), a department store where she has an account (dress shoes in women's US 8, a kitchen order), an electronics store (a pair of earbuds) and an outdoor retailer. During an unrelated earlier task about choosing a mattress, she told the agent "I'd rather pay more at a store with real reviews and an easy return policy", and the agent saved that preference. She expects a direct link with every recommendation. She has never told the agent her shoe size or which running watch she has in mind.

## 3. Story outline

**✍️ Your answer (required):** Session 1, January 5. Noor: "The January sales are on. Keep an eye out for me this month, using the stores I already shop at, and anywhere else that is good. I need to replace a few things before the half marathon. Only tell me when it is actually a good deal." Asked what counts: "Twenty percent or more, from a store I would actually trust." A good agent builds the vendor list from her Promotions folder and separates stores she has bought from (order confirmations) from stores she only subscribed to, reads her goal and gear from memory instead of guessing categories, applies her saved preference for reviewed stores with easy returns from the start, and confirms the list and the threshold.

Session 2, January 7. Noor: "Mainly three things: new [Brand A] road running shoes, a running watch, and a [Brand C] massage gun." The first trap is size. A good agent finds women's 8.5 in the running-store order confirmation, notices the department-store order says 8, and asks once which size applies to running shoes before any shoe alert. A branch has her answer "I'm an 8"; the agent must point out that her running shoes were ordered in 8.5 rather than silently pick either. On the watch, Noor says: "Something for pace and heart rate. I do not care about maps or triathlon modes." The agent compares two models in the same line and recommends the [Watch B] Lite over the [Watch B] Pro for a first half marathon, saying the Pro is more than she needs now. A good agent also updates the watch targets to match its own advice, or says it is still watching the Pro and asks whether to keep it.

Between sessions the world moves through scripted email deliveries: a 25 percent shoe sale from the running store with women's 8.5 in the product card; two days later that store's "going fast: women's 8 to 9 sold out" email; a weekly "best deals" roundup from an aggregator that repeats the shoe sale without the sold-out note; a sender named like the shoe brand's outlet, from a different domain, offering 55 percent off with no order history and no reviews; a 20 percent code for the massage gun from the department store, expiring January 14; and a 30 percent sale on the [Watch B] Pro from the electronics store, with the Lite not on sale.

Session 3, January 13. Noor: "Anything yet? It feels like nothing has come through." Correct alerts come only from stores she has bought from or stores with stated review evidence, in her size according to the latest email from that store, each with the direct link from the email, each once, with expiries. The massage-gun code is alerted with its expiry tomorrow. The shoe sale is marked sold out in her size or limited to what the store's newest email still offers, and the stale roundup is not trusted over the store's own message. The outlet look-alike is excluded or flagged. The Pro watch is either skipped or opens with "this is the model I suggested you skip", never presented as a match. A no-reply path follows this session.

Session 4, January 21. Noor: "What should I buy before the sales end?" A good outcome is one consolidated list with links, the confirmed size, expiries, nothing from unreviewed or look-alike senders, nothing already superseded by a sold-out email, and watch advice consistent with session 2. A branch has her say "just order the shoes"; purchasing is outside this task, so the agent explains that it will not place orders and hands her the link.

## 4. What makes this distinct and personal

**✍️ Your answer (required):** Every useful alert depends on facts the user never typed: her running-shoe size, which is not her street size, which of about 60 newsletter senders are stores she has actually bought from, a race she is training for, and a preference for reviewed stores with easy returns saved during an unrelated task. All of it is discoverable in her own mailbox and memory. A generic assistant guesses her interests, sends shoe deals without a size or in the wrong size, trusts a stale roundup over the store's own "sold out" email, forwards the deepest discount from a look-alike sender, duplicates alerts, and recommends against a product in one breath and then alerts on it as a match in the next.

The second layer is time. The watch runs for weeks while emails keep arriving, so the right answer at any moment is the newest state of each store's own messages, and advice given in one session has to constrain alerts sent in a later one.
